# Hyponatremia (Low Blood Sodium)

## Overview

Hyponatremia occurs when the sodium level in the blood is lower than normal. Sodium is a critical electrolyte that maintains blood pressure and enables proper functioning of nerves, muscles, and tissues. Normal range: 136–144 mEq/L (mmol/L).

## How It Develops

When sodium concentration drops in fluids surrounding cells, water shifts into the cells, causing them to swell. Brain cells are particularly vulnerable, which accounts for many symptoms.

## Causes

Three patterns:
- **Euvolemic hyponatremia** — total body water increases while sodium remains stable. SIADH is classic.
- **Hypervolemic hyponatremia** — both water and sodium increase, but water gain exceeds sodium gain. Seen in heart failure, cirrhosis, kidney disease.
- **Hypovolemic hyponatremia** — both substances are lost, but sodium loss predominates. Seen with vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, burns, diuretics.

Common triggers: diuretic medications, heart failure, kidney disease, cirrhosis, **SIADH** (syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone), burns, diarrhea, sweating, vomiting.

## Symptoms

Confusion, irritability, restlessness, convulsions, fatigue, headache, loss of appetite, muscle weakness or cramps, nausea or vomiting.

## Diagnosis

Physical exam plus blood and urine tests: basic metabolic panel, blood osmolality, comprehensive metabolic panel, urine osmolality, urine sodium.

## Treatment

Depends on underlying cause: IV fluids, symptom-relieving medications, water restriction. Correction speed matters — too rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination.

## Complications

Severe hyponatremia may cause decreased consciousness, hallucinations, coma, brain herniation, or death.
