# Vitamin D Deficiency

## Overview

Vitamin D deficiency occurs when the body is not getting enough vitamin D to stay healthy.

## Role of Vitamin D

Vitamin D aids calcium absorption, essential for bone structure. Beyond skeletal health, it plays roles in the nervous, muscle, and immune systems.

## Sources

Three pathways: natural synthesis from sunlight, dietary intake (fatty fish, beef liver, cheese, mushrooms, egg yolks; fortified milk, cereals, orange juice, yogurt, soy drinks), and supplementation.

## Daily Requirements

Vary by age: 400 IU for infants up to 800 IU for adults over 70. Pregnant and breastfeeding women require 600 IU daily. High-risk individuals may need more.

## Causes of Deficiency

- Inadequate dietary intake
- Malabsorption disorders
- Insufficient sunlight exposure
- Impaired liver or kidney function (affects activation)
- Medications interfering with absorption/metabolism

## High-Risk Populations

- Breastfed infants (human milk contains minimal vitamin D)
- Older adults (reduced skin synthesis and kidney conversion)
- People with darker skin tones
- Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, celiac disease
- Obesity or gastric bypass history
- Chronic kidney or liver disease
- Patients on cholesterol, anti-seizure, steroid, or weight-loss medications

## Diagnosis

A blood test measuring 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D).

## Treatment

Vitamin D supplements (pills or liquid). Healthcare providers determine dose, frequency, and duration based on individual needs and baseline level.

## Complications

Bone density loss → osteoporosis and fractures. In children: rickets (soft, bending bones). In adults: osteomalacia (weak bones, bone pain, muscle weakness).

## Toxicity

Excessive supplementation can cause nausea/vomiting, poor appetite, constipation, weakness, weight loss. Severe toxicity can damage kidneys and elevate blood calcium, leading to confusion, kidney failure, irregular heartbeat. Sun exposure cannot produce toxic levels.
