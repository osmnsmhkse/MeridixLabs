# Hypothyroidism

## What is Hypothyroidism?

Hypothyroidism, also called underactive thyroid, occurs when your thyroid gland doesn't make enough thyroid hormones to meet your body's needs. The thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland in the front of the neck that produces hormones controlling how the body uses energy. These hormones affect breathing, heart rate, weight, digestion, and mood. Without sufficient thyroid hormones, many body functions slow down, but treatments are available.

## What Causes Hypothyroidism?

Several factors can lead to hypothyroidism:

- Hashimoto's disease, an autoimmune disorder where the immune system attacks the thyroid (most common cause)
- Thyroiditis (thyroid inflammation)
- Congenital hypothyroidism (present at birth)
- Surgical removal of part or all of the thyroid
- Radiation treatment to the thyroid
- Certain medications
- In rare cases, pituitary disease or insufficient/excessive dietary iodine

## Who is at Risk?

Risk factors include being female, age over 60, previous thyroid problems, thyroid surgery or radiation, family history of thyroid disease, recent pregnancy or childbirth, Turner syndrome, pernicious anemia, Sjögren's syndrome, type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and lupus.

## Symptoms

Symptoms vary by person and may include fatigue, weight gain, facial puffiness, cold intolerance, joint and muscle pain, constipation, dry skin and hair, decreased sweating, heavy or irregular menstrual periods, fertility problems, depression, slowed heart rate, and goiter. Because hypothyroidism develops slowly, many people don't notice symptoms of the disease for months or even years.

## Complications

Hypothyroidism can contribute to high cholesterol. In rare cases, untreated hypothyroidism causes myxedema coma, a life-threatening condition. During pregnancy, it may cause premature birth, high blood pressure, miscarriage, and impaired fetal growth and development.

## Diagnosis

Healthcare providers use medical history, physical examination, and thyroid tests including TSH, T3, T4, thyroid antibody blood tests, and imaging tests such as thyroid scan, ultrasound, or radioactive iodine uptake testing.

## Treatment

Treatment involves hormone replacement medication (levothyroxine). After starting medication, you will get a blood test to check your thyroid hormone level within 6 to 8 weeks. The provider adjusts dosage as needed. Once the correct dose is established, testing occurs every 6 months initially, then annually. With proper medication adherence, hypothyroidism can be controlled. Pregnant women require increased iodine intake since the baby obtains iodine from maternal diet.
