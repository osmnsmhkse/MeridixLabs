# Hyperthyroidism (Overactive Thyroid)

## Definition

Hyperthyroidism occurs when your thyroid gland makes more thyroid hormones than your body needs. The thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland in the front of the neck that produces hormones regulating how the body uses energy. These hormones influence nearly every organ and control critical functions including breathing, heart rate, weight management, digestion, and mood regulation.

## Causes

- **Graves' disease** — most common cause; an autoimmune disorder where the immune system attacks the thyroid, causing excessive hormone production.
- **Thyroid nodules** — growths on the thyroid that may become overactive, particularly common in older adults.
- **Thyroiditis** — thyroid inflammation that causes stored hormones to leak from the gland.
- **Excessive iodine intake** — from medicines, cough syrups, seaweed, and supplements.
- **Medication overdose** — when patients taking thyroid hormone for hypothyroidism consume too much.

## Risk Factors

Female sex, age over 60, recent pregnancy or childbirth, prior thyroid surgery or goiter, family history of thyroid disease, pernicious anemia or vitamin B12 deficiency, type 1 diabetes, primary adrenal insufficiency, excessive dietary iodine.

## Symptoms

Nervousness, irritability, fatigue, muscle weakness, heat intolerance, insomnia, hand tremors, rapid or irregular heartbeat, frequent bowel movements, diarrhea, weight loss, mood changes. A goiter (enlarged thyroid) may cause neck swelling and difficulty breathing or swallowing. Older adults may experience appetite loss or social withdrawal, sometimes mistaken for depression or dementia.

## Diagnosis

Medical history, physical exam, thyroid blood tests (TSH, T3, T4, thyroid antibodies), imaging (thyroid scans, ultrasound, radioactive iodine uptake).

## Treatment

- **Medicines** — antithyroid drugs that reduce hormone production (typically 1–2 years), plus beta blockers for symptom relief.
- **Radioiodine therapy** — common and effective; patient ingests radioactive iodine that gradually destroys hormone-producing thyroid cells. Most patients subsequently develop hypothyroidism, which is easier to treat than hyperthyroidism.
- **Surgery** — partial or total thyroidectomy in select cases. Complete removal requires lifelong thyroid hormone replacement.

## Complications

Untreated hyperthyroidism can cause irregular heartbeat leading to blood clots, stroke, and heart failure. Graves' ophthalmopathy may cause double vision, light sensitivity, and eye pain. Other complications: bone thinning and osteoporosis, fertility problems, pregnancy complications (premature birth, low birth weight, high blood pressure, miscarriage).
