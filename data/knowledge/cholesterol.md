# Cholesterol

## What Is Cholesterol?

Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance present in all body cells. The body needs cholesterol to produce hormones, vitamin D, and digestive substances. While the body manufactures the cholesterol it needs, additional cholesterol comes from animal-based foods like egg yolks, meat, and cheese. Excessive blood cholesterol can combine with other substances to form plaque that adheres to artery walls — atherosclerosis — potentially leading to coronary artery disease.

## Types of Lipoproteins

- **HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein)** — "good" cholesterol; transports excess cholesterol back to the liver for removal.
- **LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein)** — "bad" cholesterol; elevated LDL contributes to arterial plaque.
- **VLDL (Very Low-Density Lipoprotein)** — also "bad"; primarily carries triglycerides but similarly promotes plaque.

## Causes of High Cholesterol

Most common cause is an unhealthy lifestyle:
- Saturated fats (red meat, full-fat dairy, processed foods)
- Sedentary behavior (lowers HDL)
- Smoking (reduces HDL, raises LDL)
- Chronic stress
- Excessive alcohol
- Genetics (e.g., familial hypercholesterolemia)

## Risk Factors

Advancing age, male sex (before age 40), family history, diabetes, kidney disease, certain medications (steroids, chemotherapy drugs), racial/ethnic background, overweight or obesity.

## Symptoms and Diagnosis

High cholesterol typically produces no symptoms. Diagnosis is by lipid blood panel. Screening: children ages 9–11 (initial, repeat every 5 years), adults 20–65 every 5 years (every 1–2 years for men 45–65 and women 55–65), annually after 65.

## Treatment

Lifestyle is the foundation: heart-healthy eating, weight management, regular physical activity. When lifestyle alone is insufficient, cholesterol-lowering medications are added — statins are the primary class. Lipoprotein apheresis is used for some patients with familial hypercholesterolemia.

## Complications

Untreated high cholesterol can cause plaque ruptures triggering blood clots that block coronary arteries — angina or heart attack. Plaque in other arteries can lead to carotid artery disease, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease.
