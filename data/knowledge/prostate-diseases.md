# Prostate Diseases (including PSA testing)

## The Prostate

The prostate is a gland in the male reproductive system located just below the bladder. It produces fluid contributing to semen.

## Types of Prostate Diseases

### Prostatitis
Inflammation and swelling of the prostate — the most common prostate problem in people under 50. Forms:
- **Chronic prostatitis** (chronic pelvic pain syndrome) — most prevalent.
- **Acute bacterial prostatitis** — sudden onset from infection; requires antibiotics; uncommon.
- **Chronic bacterial prostatitis** — recurring bacterial infections with gradual onset.
- **Asymptomatic prostatitis** — no symptoms; typically needs no treatment.

### Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)
Natural age-related growth of the prostate. Very common in older adults, rare under age 40. Enlargement may compress the urethra, causing urination difficulties.

### Prostate Cancer
Cancer cells forming in the prostate, predominantly affecting those 50 and older. Most cases progress slowly without causing serious harm, though some variants are aggressive.

## Risk Factors

- **Prostatitis:** lower urinary tract infections, nerve damage from surgery/injury, emotional stress.
- **BPH:** age 40+, family history, obesity, heart disease, circulation problems, type 2 diabetes, physical inactivity.
- **Prostate cancer:** age 50+, family history, African American ethnicity.

## Symptoms

Frequent urination, urgency without adequate output, leakage, weak stream. Other: complete urinary retention (medical emergency), nocturia, hematuria, fever/chills, pain during urination or ejaculation.

## Diagnosis

History and family history; physical exam including digital rectal exam (DRE); blood/urine tests; urodynamic testing; ultrasound imaging; **PSA blood test**; prostate biopsy.

### About the PSA Test

PSA (prostate-specific antigen) is a protein produced by prostate tissue. Elevated PSA can occur with BPH, prostatitis, or prostate cancer — it is not specific to cancer. Levels also vary with age and prostate volume. Interpretation requires clinical context; isolated PSA values cannot diagnose cancer.
